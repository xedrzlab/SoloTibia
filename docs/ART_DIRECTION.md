# SoloTibia — Art Direction

The visual source of truth. Every asset in the game is generated by
`scripts/generate-assets.mjs`; nothing is imported. This file records the rules
that script enforces, so new art can be checked against something written down
rather than against whatever was made most recently.

The goal is that the whole world looks like the work of one hand. Consistency
matters more than any individual asset being impressive: a simple sprite that
matches the game's visual language is worth more than a detailed one that
looks like it came from somewhere else.

---

## 1. Pixel density — the rule most likely to be broken

**One art pixel is two screen pixels.** Sprites are authored on a 16px grid
and saved at `SCALE = 2`, giving 32×32 world tiles.

This is the rule that killed the original imported art. A third-party pack
authored at one art pixel per screen pixel is not "higher quality" here — it
is a different density, and mixed with this game's art it reads as two artists
no matter how good either half is. If new art can't be produced at this
density, the answer is to redraw it, not to change the density.

A sprite may be any *size* — a building is 48×56 source, a troll 20×26 — but
the grid it is drawn on never changes.

## 2. Lighting

**One source, upper left, for everything.**

- Top faces and left faces take the highlight.
- Right faces and undersides darken.
- Contact shadows fall to the lower right.

Every object that sits in the world gets a contact shadow. Without one a
sprite reads as hovering. Shadows are pixel shapes, never blurred discs.

## 3. Perspective

Top-down, but neither flat-on nor isometric. The ground stays a square grid.
Anything with height shows a little of its front face below what you see from
above — a building shows roof over a band of wall carrying the door; a tree
shows canopy over trunk.

Sprites are anchored at the **bottom-right corner of their tile** and drawn
with `origin(1, 1)`. Extra height and width then extend up and to the left on
their own, which is where the world's characteristic lean comes from. Depth
sorting is by the anchor's tile Y, so a tall sprite overlapping the tiles
behind it still sorts correctly against anything standing there.

## 4. Palette

Colour comes from shared families, not from per-asset invention. The families
live at the top of `generate-assets.mjs`:

| Family | Constant | Used by |
|---|---|---|
| Grass | `P_GRASS` | grass tiles, foliage, moss |
| Earth | `P_DIRT` | dirt, paths, rocky ground |
| Stone | `P_STONE` | cave floor, walls, rocks, gravestones |
| Cobble | `P_COBBLE` | town plaza |
| Water | `P_WATER` | the millpond |
| Bark | `P_BARK` | trunks, posts, fences, hafts |

Saturation is reserved for what the player has to find: loot, effects, the
player themself. The environment stays subdued so it never competes with what
matters. The player's red tunic is deliberately the warmest thing on screen.

## 5. Detail

Texture is built from **pixel clusters**, not scattered single pixels. The
same pixel count placed one at a time reads as static — it is the fastest way
to make pixel art look machine-made. `clusters()` exists for this.

Before adding a pixel, ask what it says. A crack, an edge, a material change,
a highlight that describes a plane: worth it. A dot that is merely a different
colour: not.

## 6. Collision is not the sprite

A prop's blocking square is stored separately from the tile grid
(`blockedCells` in `src/data/tilemap.ts`), so a prop can block a cell without
changing the ground drawn beneath it, and a large sprite's footprint need not
match its art. A tree's canopy is wide; its trunk is what stops you.

## 6b. Layered objects

Trees are built from separate sprites — trunk, canopy, and an optional detail
— composited in `WorldScene.buildTree()`. This is not only for variety:

- **Occlusion.** A single sprite baked into the tile layer can never be walked
  behind. With the canopy as its own object sorting one step above its tile, a
  player behind the trunk is hidden by the leaves while one in front still
  draws over it.
- **Combinations.** Three canopies and three details cover far more ground
  than nine whole-tree sprites, and each addition multiplies rather than adds.

Which canopy and detail a tree gets is hashed from its tile position, so a
wood looks composed rather than rolled and is identical every time the map is
built. Anything else tall and varied should follow the same pattern.

**The player is a paper doll** built the same way: a base body wearing only a
plain shirt, with armour, helmet, weapon, shield and backpack as separate
sheets stacked over it in `PAPER_DOLL_ORDER`. Every layer shares the base
sheet's frame indices and geometry constants (`PG`), because a layer that
disagrees with the body by one pixel is glaring in motion. `ItemDef.paperDoll`
names which sheet an item draws; items that share a silhouette at this size —
a sword and an axe — share a layer.

The base body deliberately wears muted cloth. The player's identity used to be
a red tunic; that job now belongs to whatever they are actually wearing, and a
silhouette cast one pixel down-right keeps them separable from the ground when
it doesn't — plate armour on the cobbled plaza is otherwise grey on grey.

Which parts are worth splitting: things tall enough to walk behind, and things
whose pieces recombine. One-tile props, item icons and effects are neither —
splitting those is filing for its own sake.

## 8. Naming and registration

Files are filed by role with numbered variants:

```
terrain/grass_01.png       environment/tree_oak_02.png
props/cart_01.png          buildings/forge_01.png
characters/player_sheet.png  creatures/troll_sheet.png
items/weapon_sword.png     effects/hit_spark_01.png
```

Texture keys and file paths are kept separate in `src/data/assets.ts`. Game
code asks for `"tree"`; the registry says where that lives. The folder layout
can be reorganised without touching game logic, and a missing file reports
itself at load rather than showing up as a blank sprite somewhere else.

Adding art is therefore: draw it in `generate-assets.mjs`, save it to the
right folder, add one line to `assets.ts`.

## 9. Animation

Directional sheets are laid out as four frames per direction, in the order
`down, left, right, up` (`DIRECTION_ORDER`). Within a direction: frame 0 idle,
frames 1 and 2 the alternating walk steps, frame 3 the attack.

Walk cycles live in the legs. Moving the whole sprite up and down is not
animation. An attack must read from the silhouette alone.

## 10. Checks before accepting a new asset

1. Is it on the 16px grid?
2. Does the light come from the upper left?
3. Does it have a contact shadow?
4. Are its colours from an existing family?
5. Is its texture clusters, not scattered pixels?
6. Is it the right scale beside the player?
7. Does it read at gameplay zoom, in a busy scene — not just enlarged on its own?
8. Is its anchor at the base, where it meets the ground?

Check 7 has caught more problems than the rest combined. Render tiles as a 2×2
block to see how they repeat, and put characters next to the player in-game
before trusting them.

## 11. Known gaps

Honest list of what the direction asks for and the game does not yet do:

- **Weather** — no rain, snow or fog.
- **Item rarity** — rarity has no visual language yet; items are told apart by
  silhouette and material only.
- **Typography** — the UI uses the system monospace font, not a pixel font.
- **Audio** — no sound at all, so none of the audio-visual pairing applies yet.
- **Biome transition tiles** — regions meet at a hard edge rather than blending.
